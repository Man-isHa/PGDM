From solving equations to fitting data

Motivating example: house prices

Predict a new house price

How much for a 1750 sqft house?
\[\hat y=0.04\times1750=\boxed{70\text{ lakhs}}\]
We implicitly assumed a linear relationship.

The equation of a line

\[\boxed{\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x}\]
\(\beta_0\)intercept
\(\beta_1\)slope
\(x\)input feature
\(\hat y\)predicted response

What if we have multiple features?

\[\hat y=\beta_0+\beta_1x_1+\cdots+\beta_dx_d\]
Two features: the line becomes a plane. Size, age → price.

Linear regression in matrix form

\[\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&x_{11}&\cdots&x_{1d}\\\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\1&x_{n1}&\cdots&x_{nd}\end{bmatrix}}_{X\in\mathbb R^{n\times(d+1)}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\\\vdots\\\beta_d\end{bmatrix}}_{\beta}\approx\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\\vdots\\y_n\end{bmatrix}}_{y}\]

The intercept as a feature

For \(x_i\in\mathbb R^d\), define
\[\tilde x_i=\begin{bmatrix}1\\x_i\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{d+1}\]
Row \(i\) of \(X\) is \(\tilde x_i^\top\).

Exercise 1: find the parameters

\[\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix}\]
Find \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\).
3 minutes

Exercise 1: solution

\[\beta_0=1,\qquad\beta_0+\beta_1=3\]
\[\boxed{\beta_0=1,\quad\beta_1=2}\]
Column picture: 1 × column 1 + 2 × column 2 lands exactly on y.

When is the solution unique?

For a square system \(X\beta=y\):
\[\det(X)\neq0\ \Longrightarrow\ \beta=X^{-1}y\]
What if there are more observations than parameters?

Exercise 2: more equations than unknowns

\[\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{bmatrix}\]
Can all three equations hold exactly?
5 minutes

Why an exact solution does not exist

The first two equations imply
\[\beta_0=1,\qquad\beta_1=1\]
The third would then require
\[1+2(1)=2\quad\text{✗}\]

Instead, minimise the error

Residual vector
\[r(\beta)=y-X\beta\]
Ordinary least squares
\[\hat\beta\in\arg\min_{\beta}\|y-X\beta\|_2^2\]

Ordinary least squares

\[\|y-X\beta\|_2^2=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\tilde x_i^\top\beta)^2\]
The total area of the squares.

Exercise 2: best-fitting line

\[(x_i,y_i)\in\{(0,1),(1,2),(2,2)\}\]
Find the line with minimum squared error.
\[\boxed{\hat y=\tfrac76+\tfrac12x}\]

Deriving the OLS solution

\[L(\beta)=(y-X\beta)^\top(y-X\beta)\]
\[\nabla_\beta L(\beta)=-2X^\top(y-X\beta)\]

The normal equations

\[X^\top(y-X\hat\beta)=0\]
\[\boxed{X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y}\]

The closed-form OLS estimator

If \(\operatorname{rank}(X)=d+1\),
\[\boxed{\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y}\]
Why do we need full column rank?

The geometry of least squares

\[\hat y=X\hat\beta\in\operatorname{col}(X)\]
\[\boxed{\hat r=y-\hat y\ \perp\ \operatorname{col}(X)}\]
\(\hat y\) is the orthogonal projection of \(y\).
Drag to rotate

The projection matrix

\[\hat y=P_Xy,\qquad\boxed{P_X=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top}\]
\[P_X^2=P_X,\qquad P_X^\top=P_X\]
Projecting twice changes nothing.

A geometric check

\[X^\top\hat r=0\]
Are residuals orthogonal to every feature column?
Yes, including the intercept column: \(\sum_i\hat r_i=0\).

Multicollinearity and identifiability

When the coefficients stop being unique

What if \(X^\top X\) is singular?

\[\det(X^\top X)=0\]
The inverse formula cannot be used.
Does this mean predictions are impossible?

Exercise 3: dependent features

\[X=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\color{#6A4BC4}{2}\\1&2&\color{#6A4BC4}{4}\\1&3&\color{#6A4BC4}{6}\end{bmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{bmatrix}2\\3\\4\end{bmatrix}\]
Column 3 is twice column 2.

Exercise 3: two different solutions

\[\beta^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\beta^{(2)}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix}\]
\[\boxed{X\beta^{(1)}=X\beta^{(2)}=y}\]
Bars: β₀ (grey), β₁x₁ (blue), β₂x₂ (violet). Amber: prediction.

Identifiability

\(\beta\) is identifiable from \(X\beta\) if
\[X\beta=X\beta'\ \Longrightarrow\ \beta=\beta'\]
This holds precisely when \(\operatorname{null}(X)=\{0\}\).

Why predictions can survive

If \(v\in\operatorname{null}(X)\), then
\[X(\beta+v)=X\beta\]
Different coefficients, identical fitted values.
Here \(v=(0,-2,1)\).

How do we fit a singular system?

\[\boxed{\hat\beta=X^+y}\]
Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse
The minimum-norm least-squares solution.

The probabilistic view

Maximum likelihood and coefficient uncertainty

A second view of linear regression

\[\boxed{y_i=\tilde x_i^\top\beta+\varepsilon_i}\]
What should we assume about \(\varepsilon_i\)?

Gaussian noise model

\[\varepsilon_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\]
\[Y_i\mid x_i\sim\mathcal N(\tilde x_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2)\]

Likelihood of one observation

\[p(y_i\mid x_i,\beta,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i-\tilde x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]
Large prediction errors are less likely.

Likelihood of the dataset

By conditional independence,
\[L(\beta,\sigma^2)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_i\mid x_i,\beta,\sigma^2)\]
Why is taking a logarithm helpful?
Products become sums; tiny numbers stop underflowing.

Log-likelihood

\[\log L=\underbrace{-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)}_{\text{no }\beta}\ \underbrace{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\tilde x_i^\top\beta)^2}_{\text{squared error}}\]
Which term depends on \(\beta\)?

Exercise 4: MLE vs OLS

For fixed \(\sigma^2>0\):
\[\arg\max_\beta\log L=\arg\min_\beta\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\tilde x_i^\top\beta)^2\]
\[\boxed{\hat\beta_{\rm MLE}=\hat\beta_{\rm OLS}}\]

Does squared loss always apply?

Gaussian errors → squared error
Laplace errors → absolute error
The noise assumption determines the loss.

Near multicollinearity

Suppose \(x_2\approx2x_1\).
\(X^\top X\) invertible but ill-conditioned.
Small changes in data, large changes in coefficients.
200 datasets, true β = (1, 1). Each dot is one dataset’s estimate.

What is \(R_j^2\) in the VIF?

Original model:
\[y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_{i1}+\beta_2x_{i2}+\beta_3x_{i3}+\varepsilon_i\]
How much of \(x_2\) can the other features explain?
Fit an auxiliary regression:
\[x_{i2}=\alpha_0+\alpha_1x_{i1}+\alpha_3x_{i3}+u_i\]
\[\boxed{R_2^2=1-\frac{\sum_{i}(x_{i2}-\hat x_{i2})^2}{\sum_{i}(x_{i2}-\bar x_2)^2}}\]
  • \(R_2^2=0\): other features explain none of the variation in \(x_2\).
  • \(R_2^2=0.96\): they explain 96% of it.
  • \(R_2^2=1\): \(x_2\) is perfectly explained by the others.
We are predicting \(x_2\), not the original target \(y\)!

Variance inflation factor (VIF)

\[\boxed{\mathrm{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}}\]
Why it matters. Under the classical homoscedastic model,
\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X)=\underbrace{\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2}}_{\text{variance without collinearity}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}}_{\mathrm{VIF}_j}\]
\(R_2^2=0.96\ \Rightarrow\ \mathrm{VIF}_2=\dfrac{1}{1-0.96}=\boxed{25}\)
  • variance of \(\hat\beta_2\) inflated × 25
  • standard error inflated × \(\sqrt{25}=5\)
What if \(R_2^2=1\)? \(\ \mathrm{VIF}_2=\infty\ \Longrightarrow\ \) \(\beta_2\) is not uniquely identifiable.

Variance of OLS coefficients

If \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I\) and \(X\) has full column rank,
\[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}}\]
Near-collinearity makes some diagonal entries large.

Variance inflation factor

Regress \(x_j\) on the other features; get \(R_j^2\).
\[\boxed{\mathrm{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}}\]

Exercise 5: calculate the VIF

Suppose \(R_j^2=0.96\).
\[\mathrm{VIF}_j=\frac{1}{1-0.96}=\boxed{25}\]
Variance × 25, standard error × 5.

Can we trust our regression model?

Standard errors, \(t\)-tests, confidence intervals and diagnostics

A question about the coefficients

Suppose linear regression estimates
\[\boxed{\hat\beta_1=2.4}\]
Does this tell us that the true coefficient is 2.4?
No. We estimated \(\beta_1\) from a finite, noisy sample.
What would happen if we collected a different sample?

Different samples, different estimates

Repeat the same study with new observations:
Sample12345
\(\hat\beta_1\)2.42.12.72.32.6
The estimate changes from sample to sample.
\(\hat\beta_1\) is a random variable; \(\beta_1\) is the fixed, unknown parameter.
Numbers are illustrative.

The statistical model

\[\boxed{y=X\beta+\varepsilon}\]
\(X\in\mathbb R^{n\times p}\) has full column rank.
\[\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I_n\]
  • \(\beta\in\mathbb R^p\): true, unknown coefficients
  • \(\varepsilon\): random errors with common variance \(\sigma^2\)

Why is the OLS estimate random?

\[\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\]
Substitute \(y=X\beta+\varepsilon\):
\[\hat\beta=\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon\]
\[\boxed{\hat\beta-\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon}\]
Random errors produce random estimates.

Mean and variance of the OLS estimator

With \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\),
\[\boxed{\mathbb E[\hat\beta\mid X]=\beta}\]
With \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I_n\),
\[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}}\]
Diagonal entry \(j\): \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X)=\sigma^2\big[(X^\top X)^{-1}\big]_{jj}\)

But we do not know \(\sigma^2\)

\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X)=\color{#C92A52}{\sigma^2}\big[(X^\top X)^{-1}\big]_{jj}\]
contains the unknown noise variance.
Estimate it from the residuals: \(\ \hat\varepsilon_i=y_i-\hat y_i\)
Can we estimate \(\sigma^2\) from the squared residuals?

Estimating the noise variance

\[\mathrm{RSS}=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\]
\[\boxed{s^2=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-p}}\]
\(p\) = number of fitted coefficients, including the intercept.
Why \(n-p\)? Fitting \(p\) coefficients uses \(p\) degrees of freedom.
Under the stated model, \(s^2\) is unbiased for \(\sigma^2\).

Standard error: definition

The standard error estimates the standard deviation of a coefficient estimator across repeated samples.
\[\boxed{SE(\hat\beta_j)=\sqrt{s^2\big[(X^\top X)^{-1}\big]_{jj}}}\]
  • Small SE ⇒ estimated more precisely
  • Large SE ⇒ estimated less precisely

A small standard-error calculation

\[s^2=4,\qquad\big[(X^\top X)^{-1}\big]_{11}=0.16\]
Exercise (1 minute): find \(SE(\hat\beta_1)\).
\[SE(\hat\beta_1)=\sqrt{4\times0.16}=\sqrt{0.64}\]
\[\boxed{SE(\hat\beta_1)=0.8}\]
The SE measures uncertainty, not the coefficient’s size.

Two estimates: which is more convincing?

Study AStudy B
\(\hat\beta_1\)2.42.4
\(SE(\hat\beta_1)\)0.82.0
Which gives stronger evidence that the true coefficient differs from zero?
Study A: its estimate has a smaller standard error.

Hypothesis testing for a coefficient

Does feature \(x_j\) have a nonzero partial regression coefficient?
\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}H_0&:\beta_j=0\\H_1&:\beta_j\neq0\end{aligned}}\]
Important: we test the true \(\beta_j\), not whether \(\hat\beta_j\) equals zero.

The \(t\)-statistic

To test \(H_0:\beta_j=b_0\),
\[\boxed{t=\frac{\hat\beta_j-b_0}{SE(\hat\beta_j)}}\]
For the common null \(H_0:\beta_j=0\),
\[\boxed{t=\frac{\hat\beta_j}{SE(\hat\beta_j)}}\]
How many standard errors the estimate is from the null value.

Exercise: compute the \(t\)-statistic

\[\hat\beta_1=2.4,\qquad SE(\hat\beta_1)=0.8\]
Exercise (2 minutes): test \(H_0:\beta_1=0\).
\[t=\frac{2.4-0}{0.8}=\boxed{3}\]
The estimate is 3 standard errors from zero.
But is \(t=3\) large enough to reject \(H_0\)?

Why a \(t\)-distribution?

Classical model, Gaussian errors:
\[\frac{\hat\beta_j-\beta_j}{SE(\hat\beta_j)}\sim t_{n-p}\]
  • \(n\): observations
  • \(p\): fitted coefficients
  • \(n-p\): residual degrees of freedom
Heavier tails than the normal because \(\sigma^2\) is estimated.

When do we reject the null hypothesis?

\(n=25,\ p=2\):
\[\text{df}=n-p=23\]
5% level, two-sided: \(\ \pm t_{0.975,23}\approx\pm2.069\)
\[\boxed{|3|>2.069}\]
Reject \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) at the 5% level.

What is a \(p\)-value?

The probability, assuming \(H_0\), of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.
\[\boxed{p\text{-value}=P(|T_{23}|\ge3)\approx0.0064}\]
\(0.0064<0.05\), so we reject \(H_0\).
A \(p\)-value is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true.

Confidence intervals: a different question

A \(t\)-test asks: “Is the true coefficient plausibly zero?”
A confidence interval asks: “Which coefficient values are compatible with the data?”
\[\boxed{\hat\beta_j\ \pm\ t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-p}\,SE(\hat\beta_j)}\]

Exercise: construct a 95% confidence interval

\(n=25,\ p=2,\ t_{0.975,23}\approx2.069,\quad\hat\beta_1=2.4,\ SE(\hat\beta_1)=0.8\)
Exercise (2 minutes): find the 95% interval.
\[2.4\pm2.069\times0.8=2.4\pm1.6552\]
\[\boxed{CI_{95\%}\approx[0.745,\ 4.055]}\]

What does “95% confidence” mean?

Repeatedly collect new samples; build an interval from each.
About 95% of these intervals contain the fixed, true \(\beta_j\).
It does not mean that, after seeing one interval, \(\beta_j\) has a 95% probability of lying inside it.

\(t\)-tests and confidence intervals agree

We tested \(H_0:\beta_1=0\) with estimate 2.4 and SE 0.8.
\[\boxed{CI_{95\%}=[0.745,\ 4.055]}\]
Does the interval contain 0?
No, so the two-sided \(t\)-test rejects \(H_0\) at 5%.
Rejecting \(H_0\) ⇔ the null value lies outside the CI.

Connection to multicollinearity

\[\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_j\mid X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2}\underbrace{\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}}_{\mathrm{VIF}_j}\]
Higher VIF ⇒ larger SE, holding everything else fixed.
An imprecisely estimated coefficient may not be statistically significant.

Does statistical significance mean causation?

Education is significant in a regression predicting salary.
Have we proved that education causes higher salary?
No. Omitted variables and confounding can explain the association.
Significance ≠ causal identification ≠ practical importance.

Assumptions: when does OLS work?

For the familiar OLS formulas, assume:
  1. \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\): zero conditional mean
  2. \(\operatorname{rank}(X)=p\): no perfect multicollinearity
Then OLS is unbiased conditional on \(X\).
A high \(R^2\) does not verify these assumptions.

Additional assumptions for classical inference

For the conventional variance formula: \(\ \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I_n\)
  • Homoscedasticity: constant conditional error variance
  • Uncorrelated errors: zero conditional covariance across observations
For exact small-sample \(t\)-tests and intervals, also:
\[\varepsilon\mid X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_n)\]

Residual diagnostics

\[\boxed{e_i=y_i-\hat y_i}\]
Plot \(e_i\) against fitted \(\hat y_i\).
  • Random scatter near zero is reassuring.
  • Systematic shapes reveal problems.
What patterns should we look for?

Residual plot A: no clear pattern

No obvious curvature or change in spread.

Residual plot B: curved pattern

Possible misspecification of the conditional mean (e.g. missing nonlinearity).

Residual plot C: funnel shape

Possible heteroscedasticity: error variance changes with the fitted value.

Can residual plots prove the model is correct?

No. Residual plots are diagnostic tools, not proofs.
  • Curvature ⇒ possible mean-model misspecification
  • Funnel ⇒ possible unequal error variance
Other checks are needed for dependence and error normality.
A visually random residual plot does not establish causality.

Takeaways

  1. OLS is an orthogonal projection.
  2. Gaussian MLE gives the same coefficients.
  3. Good predictions do not guarantee identifiable coefficients.
  4. Reliable inference requires assumptions and diagnostics.
