Feature scaling

Why do the units of features matter?

A question: do feature scales matter?

FeatureExampleTypical range
Income (₹ lakhs/year)100–20
Credit score700500–900
With Euclidean distance, which feature dominates?
20 score points ≫ 2 income units, numerically.

Why can this be a problem?

\[A=(10,700),\ B=(12,700),\ C=(10,720)\]
\[d(A,B)=2,\qquad d(A,C)=20\]
So \(B\) looks much closer to \(A\).
Is 2 lakhs really less important than 20 score points?
Distance depends on units and scales.

What is feature scaling?

Transform numerical features so their magnitudes are comparable.
Min–max scaling
map training values into \([0,1]\)
Standardisation
centre at 0, divide by standard deviation
Why: stop large scales dominating distances; better conditioning for gradient methods.

Min–max scaling

\[x_j^{\min}=\min_ix_{ij},\qquad x_j^{\max}=\max_ix_{ij}\]
\[\boxed{\tilde x_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}-x_j^{\min}}{x_j^{\max}-x_j^{\min}}}\]
Smallest → 0, largest → 1, all training values in \([0,1]\).

Try it: min–max scaling

Annual income (₹ lakhs): \(\ x=[5,10,15]\)
Exercise (2 minutes): scale to \([0,1]\).
\[x^{\min}=5,\ x^{\max}=15:\quad\tilde x=\Big[\tfrac{5-5}{10},\ \tfrac{10-5}{10},\ \tfrac{15-5}{10}\Big]\]
\[\boxed{\tilde x=[0,\ 0.5,\ 1]}\]

Min–max scaling: what to remember

  • Preserves the ordering of values
  • Common range for nonconstant features
  • Sensitive to extreme min and max
Training range \([5,15]\): what happens to a new \(x=20\)?
\(\tilde x=\frac{20-5}{15-5}=1.5\)
New observations need not stay inside \([0,1]\).

Standardisation (z-score)

\[\bar x_j=\frac1n\sum_ix_{ij},\qquad s_j=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(x_{ij}-\bar x_j)^2}\]
\[\boxed{\tilde x_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}-\bar x_j}{s_j}}\]
Standardised training feature: mean 0, standard deviation 1.

Try it: standardisation

\(x=[8,10,12]\)
Exercise (2 minutes): standardise.
\[\bar x=10,\qquad s=\sqrt{\frac{(8-10)^2+0^2+(12-10)^2}{3-1}}=2\]
\[\boxed{\tilde x=[-1,\ 0,\ 1]}\]

How to interpret a standardised value

\[\tilde x=\frac{x-\bar x}{s}\]
\(\tilde x=0\): equals the mean
\(\tilde x=1\): one sd above
\(\tilde x=-2\): two sd below
Unlike min–max, not bounded.

Min–max vs standardisation

Min–maxStandardisation
Formula\(\frac{x-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}}\)\(\frac{x-\bar x}{s}\)
Training range\([0,1]\)unbounded
Training meannot fixed0
Training sdnot fixed1
Does either remove outliers?
No. Both remain sensitive to extreme observations.

Is scaling always necessary?

  • Distance-based (k-NN): scaling changes the nearest neighbours.
  • Gradient-based optimisation: better conditioning and convergence.
  • OLS with an intercept: predictions unchanged under affine rescaling.
Scaling changes the representation, not the information.

Area under the ROC curve (AUC)

AUC = area under the ROC curve.
\[\mathrm{AUC}=\underbrace{\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12}_{\text{first step}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\cdot1}_{\text{second step}}=\boxed{0.75}\]
Why no need to commit to one threshold?

AUC: a probability interpretation

\(S^+\): score of a random positive; \(S^-\): a random negative.
\[\boxed{\mathrm{AUC}=\Pr(S^+>S^-)+\tfrac12\Pr(S^+=S^-)}\]
Continuous scores: \(\ \mathrm{AUC}=\Pr(S^+>S^-)\)

Exercise: AUC by pairwise comparisons

Positives: 0.90, 0.60
Negatives: 0.70, 0.20
Exercise (2 minutes): how many of the 4 pairs have the positive higher?
\(0.90>0.70,\ 0.90>0.20,\ 0.60>0.20\)
\[\boxed{\mathrm{AUC}=\tfrac34=0.75}\]

What does AUC tell us?

  • AUC = 1: perfect ranking
  • AUC = 0.5: chance-level ranking
  • Measures ranking, not calibration
Does AUC = 0.9 mean probabilities are right 90% of the time?
No.

Precision and recall

When positives are rare

Precision and recall

Precision: of predicted positives, how many are right?
\[\boxed{\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}}\]
Recall: of actual positives, how many were found?
\[\boxed{\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}}\]
Recall is the same as TPR.

Exercise: precision and recall

\[TP=18,\quad FP=6,\quad FN=2\]
Exercise (1 minute): precision and recall?
\[\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{18}{24}=\boxed{0.75},\qquad\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{18}{20}=\boxed{0.90}\]

What is a precision–recall curve?

For each threshold \(\tau\):
\[(\mathrm{Recall}(\tau),\ \mathrm{Precision}(\tau))\]
  • x-axis: recall
  • y-axis: precision
  • one point per threshold
The curve shows the precision–recall trade-off.

Class imbalance: a screening example

1,000 patients: 20 with disease, 980 without.
\[TP=18,\ FN=2,\ FP=49,\ TN=931\]
Is the false-positive rate small?

ROC can look good under imbalance

\[\mathrm{TPR}=\tfrac{18}{20}=0.90,\qquad\mathrm{FPR}=\tfrac{49}{980}=0.05\]
\[\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{18}{18+49}=\boxed{0.269}\]
Only about 27% of flagged patients are sick.

Why precision–recall helps under imbalance

Prevalence \(\pi=\Pr(Y=1)\):
\[\boxed{\mathrm{Precision}=\frac{\pi\,\mathrm{TPR}}{\pi\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}}}\]
Small \(\pi\): even a modest FPR floods us with false alarms.
PR focuses on the reliability of positive predictions.

Choosing the threshold

Costs and the Bayes decision rule

Why is threshold 0.5 not always best?

Errors can have unequal consequences.
False negative
a sick patient is missed: costly
False positive
a healthy patient gets a follow-up: cheaper
Should we still predict positive only when \(p\ge0.5\)?

Costs of classification errors

\[C_{FP}>0:\ \text{false-positive cost},\qquad C_{FN}>0:\ \text{false-negative cost}\]
Correct decisions cost nothing.
\(Y=1\)\(Y=0\)
predict 10\(C_{FP}\)
predict 0\(C_{FN}\)0

Expected cost: predict positive

\(p=\Pr(Y=1\mid X=x)\), the true conditional probability.
Predict 1: the only possible error is a false positive.
\[\boxed{R(1\mid x)=(1-p)\,C_{FP}}\]
Why multiply by \(1-p\)?

Expected cost: predict negative

\[\boxed{R(0\mid x)=p\,C_{FN}}\]
\[\hat y(x)=\arg\min_{a\in\{0,1\}}R(a\mid x)\]
The Bayes decision principle.

Deriving the optimal threshold

Predict 1 when \(\ (1-p)C_{FP}\le p\,C_{FN}\)
\(C_{FP}\le p(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\)
\[\boxed{p\ge\tau^*=\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}}\]
Assumes calibrated probabilities and zero cost for correct decisions.

Exercise: a medical screening decision

\[C_{FP}=1,\qquad C_{FN}=10\]
Exercise (2 minutes): optimal threshold? Classify \(p=0.20\)?
\[\tau^*=\tfrac1{11}\approx\boxed{0.091}\]
\[0.20>0.091\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\hat y=1}\]

Check the decision by expected cost

\(p=0.20,\ C_{FP}=1,\ C_{FN}=10\)
\[R(1\mid x)=0.8\times1=0.8\]
\[R(0\mid x)=0.2\times10=2\]
Predict positive: \(0.8<2\).

Optimal threshold: what changes it?

\[\tau^*=\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP}+C_{FN}}\]
Higher \(C_{FN}\) ⇒ lower threshold.
Higher \(C_{FP}\) ⇒ higher threshold.
Caution: inaccurate probabilities ⇒ \(\tau^*\) need not minimise actual cost.

Calibration

Do the probabilities mean what they say?

Ranking versus probability calibration

100 patients, each assigned \(p=0.8\).
About how many should experience the event?
About 80 of 100, on average.
\[\Pr(Y=1\mid\hat p=p)=p\]
(a calibrated forecast)

How to build a reliability diagram

Group held-out predictions into bins \(B_b\).
\[\bar p_b=\frac{1}{|B_b|}\sum_{i\in B_b}\hat p_i,\qquad\bar y_b=\frac{1}{|B_b|}\sum_{i\in B_b}y_i\]
Plot \(\bar y_b\) against \(\bar p_b\).
Calibrated bins lie near the diagonal.

What does calibration not tell us?

  • A calibrated model need not distinguish individuals well.
  • A high-AUC model can be poorly calibrated.
  • Reliability diagrams depend on bin and sample sizes.
Predict the prevalence for everyone: calibrated, but AUC 0.5?
Yes.

Platt scaling: a parametric fix

\(s(x)\in\mathbb R\): an uncalibrated score.
Fit \(a,b\) on a separate calibration set:
\[\boxed{q(x)=\sigma(a\,s(x)+b)}\]
Fit \((a,b)\) by minimising binary cross-entropy.
Learns a sigmoid map from scores to probabilities.

Platt scaling: what can it correct?

Scores that are too extreme can be made less extreme.
  • Only two parameters to estimate
  • Assumes a sigmoid-shaped calibration map
  • Needs labelled data not used to fit the base model

Isotonic regression: a flexible fix

\[\boxed{q(x)=g(s(x)),\quad g\ \text{nondecreasing}}\]
\[\min_g\sum_{i=1}^m\big(y_i-g(s_i)\big)^2\]
No sigmoid shape assumed.
