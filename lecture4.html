Generalisation

Will the model work on new data?

How do we know if a model is good?

Fit \(\hat f\) on \(n\) training observations.
\[\widehat R_{\rm train}(\hat f)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell\big(\hat f(x_i),y_i\big)\]
Will it work on new, unseen data?

Training error is not the final goal

Squared loss: \(\ \ell(\hat y,y)=(y-\hat y)^2\)
Population risk of a fitted model:
\[\boxed{R(\hat f)=\mathbb E_{(X,Y)\sim P}\big[(Y-\hat f(X))^2\big]}\]
Training error we can compute; population risk is unknown.

A perfect training fit?

\[\widehat R_{\rm train}=0\]
Does this imply \(R(\hat f)=0\)?
No. Training data are not future data.

How many samples go into validation?

Only 100 labelled observations.
How many should we hold out to estimate the risk?
Too few: the risk estimate is noisy.
Too many: little is left for training, so the model itself gets worse.
Can every observation take a turn as validation data?

Solution: \(K\)-fold cross-validation

Split the model-development data into \(K\) disjoint folds:
\[\mathcal D=V_1\cup\cdots\cup V_K\]
For each \(k=1,\ldots,K\):
  1. train on \(\mathcal D\setminus V_k\)
  2. evaluate on \(V_k\)
Finally: average the validation losses.

5-fold cross-validation

Every observation appears in validation exactly once.

The cross-validation estimate

\(\hat f_{-k}\): trained without fold \(V_k\).
\[L_k=\frac{1}{|V_k|}\sum_{i\in V_k}\ell\big(\hat f_{-k}(x_i),y_i\big)\]
Equal fold sizes:
\[\boxed{\widehat R_{\rm CV}=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^KL_k}\]

Exercise: compute the CV error

Five equal folds, validation MSEs:
\[\boxed{8,\ 10,\ 9,\ 11,\ 12}\]
Find the 5-fold CV MSE. (2 minutes)
\[\widehat R_{\rm CV}=\frac{8+10+9+11+12}{5}=\boxed{10}\]

Why is CV useful?

  • Each observation is used for validation once.
  • Performance is assessed across several held-out folds.
  • No reliance on one arbitrary split.
Caution: CV is not guaranteed to have lower variance than every single-split estimate.

Model complexity

Underfitting, overfitting and learning curves

Model complexity: a concrete example

True relationship is curved:
\[Y=3+2X+3X^2+\varepsilon\]
Fit polynomials of degree \(d\):
\[\hat f_d(x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\cdots+\beta_dx^d\]
Does larger \(d\) always help on unseen data?

Three fits to the same observations

Which fits the training data best? Which would generalise best?

Underfitting

Too restrictive to capture important patterns.
Symptoms:
  • high training error
  • high validation error
Example: a line for strongly quadratic data.

Overfitting

Fits sample-specific noise that fails to generalise.
Symptoms:
  • very low training error
  • much higher validation error
Example: a high-degree polynomial through almost every point.

Bias and variance

Why models behave differently across samples

Why do models behave differently across samples?

Same algorithm, different datasets:
\[\mathcal D_1\to\hat f_{\mathcal D_1},\qquad\mathcal D_2\to\hat f_{\mathcal D_2}\]
Do predictions at the same \(x\) differ?
Yes. This is where bias and variance start.

What is bias?

\[f_*(x)=\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]\]
\[\bar f(x)=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\big[\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)\big]\]
\[\boxed{\operatorname{Bias}(x)=\bar f(x)-f_*(x)}\]
Systematic offset of the average fit from the truth.

What is variance?

At a fixed \(x\), different samples give different predictions.
\[\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal D}\big(\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)\big)=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\big[(\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)-\bar f(x))^2\big]}\]
High variance: the fit is sensitive to the training sample.

Bias and variance: intuition

Each dot: a fitted prediction from a different training sample. Centre: \(f_*(x)\).

Irreducible noise

\[Y=f_*(X)+\varepsilon,\qquad\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\]
\[\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X=x)=\sigma^2(x)\]
Even knowing \(f_*(x)\) exactly cannot predict fresh noise.

Expected test error at a fixed \(x\)

Squared loss, an independent new label \(Y\):
\[\mathbb E_{\mathcal D,Y\mid x}\big[(Y-\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x))^2\big]\]
Add and subtract the average fit \(\bar f(x)\):
\[Y-\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)=\underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{noise}}+\underbrace{(f_*(x)-\bar f(x))}_{\text{bias term}}+\underbrace{(\bar f(x)-\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x))}_{\text{variance term}}\]

Bias–variance decomposition

Square and take expectations.
The cross terms vanish: the random deviations have mean zero.
\[\boxed{\mathbb E\big[(Y-\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x))^2\big]=\operatorname{Bias}(x)^2+\operatorname{Var}_{\mathcal D}\big(\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)\big)+\sigma^2(x)}\]
■ bias²■ variance■ noise

The classical bias–variance trade-off

More flexibility often gives:
Lower bias: more patterns representable
Higher variance: fits vary more across samples
Minimise expected test error, not training error.

A final practical question

Very low train error, high CV error.
What might help?
  • Reduce model complexity
  • Collect more representative training data
  • Check for leakage or distribution shift
